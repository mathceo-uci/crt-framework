    Section Dimension 2: Relationships and caring

    This dimension entails promoting positive relations between mentors and students, and among students. The process starts with mentors’ actions and dispositions, which lead to a safe environment that allows for relationships to grow. Mentors have a “beyond teacher” mindset, and actively look for opportunities to connect with students at a deeper level in a two-way relationship.
    🔗
    One important difference between the after-school and in-school settings is that in an after-school program there is potentially more room to recognize the individuality of the student and support them holistically, in many different dimensions. One way this manifests is learning about the student — the idea is that we learn about the student at a deeper level than what they do mathematically or in school.
    🔗
    We have found that students create deep bonds with the mentors they work with (which takes some time), and see them as a source of external support. In this way, mentors teach, by modeling, socio-emotional skills to students in addition to mathematical skills. In fact, understanding the student at this deeper level makes students more comfortable challenging themselves and lets mentors draw out more mathematical skill from students. Students are also more inclined to share personal challenging or traumatic events with mentors, who can then relay critical information to program leaders and teachers to make sure students get the support they need.
    🔗

    Subsection Mentors’ Beliefs and Attitudes

    • I believe that every student has unique strengths that deserve celebration.
      🔗
    • I believe that building trust and care is as important as teaching content.
      🔗
    • I believe that mistakes can turn into opportunities for growth, not signs of failure.
      🔗
    • I believe that listening carefully to students helps me understand their perspectives.
      🔗
    • I believe that learning is more meaningful when students feel supported.
      🔗
    • I believe that when I model skills, it inspires students to think deeply about themselves.
      🔗
    • I believe that all students can achieve learning goals.
      🔗
    • I believe that being approachable makes students feel safe to take risks.
      🔗
    • I believe that reflecting on experiences fosters personal growth.
      🔗
    • I believe that celebrating small successes motivates students to challenge themselves.
      🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Component 1: Welcome every student

    Use your words and actions to create a positive, welcoming space where meaningful learning can happen.
    🔗

    Mentors’ Actions.

    • Greet students individually when they arrive to make them feel seen.
      🔗
    • Smile, laugh, and make eye contact throughout the session.
      🔗
    • Use positive gestures that are culturally appropriate, relevant, and respectful to show care and excitement (examples: pat on the back, high five, fist pumps — always ask first if unsure).
      🔗
    • Listen carefully to students’ ideas, respond with enthusiasm, and celebrate and acknowledge their contributions.
      🔗
    • Learn and consistently use students’ names the way they want to be called.
      🔗
    • Interact with care, respect, and compassion, and react to disrespectful behavior.
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Students’ Perspectives.

    • “I like coming here because my mentor always makes me feel welcome.”
      🔗
    • “I feel happy when we start and everyone says hi.”
      🔗
    • “My mentor remembers my name, and that makes me feel special.”
      🔗
    • “I feel safe because the mentor is nice and positive.”
      🔗
    • “It’s fun when the mentor jokes around and smiles with us.”
      🔗
    • “I can share about my own interests and experiences.”
      🔗
    • “I feel safe and respected during the program.”
      🔗
    • “I feel that my mentors care about me.”
      🔗
    • “I feel that my ideas and suggestions are valued by everyone.”
      🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Component 2: Find ways to connect

    Encourage meaningful and constant two-way exchanges with students.
    🔗

    Mentors’ Actions.

    • Show through words and body language that you are approachable, responsive, and care about each student.
      🔗
    • Share small, relatable stories or experiences to connect with students.
      🔗
    • Remember and refer to students’ personal stories, interests, and feelings.
      🔗
    • Check in regularly on students’ progress and struggles, offering emotional support.
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Students’ Perspectives.

    • “My mentor actually listens when I talk and remembers stuff I said before.”
      🔗
    • “I feel like they really care about what’s going on in my life.”
      🔗
    • “I can tell my mentor about things I like and they get it.”
      🔗
    • “I like it when we talk about stuff beyond the content of the meeting.”
      🔗
    • “I feel comfortable asking questions because my mentor is friendly.”
      🔗
    • “My mentor shares interesting stories with us!”
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Component 3: Build confidence and trust

    Help students trust themselves, you, and the learning process.
    🔗

    Mentors’ Actions.

    • Be mindful of students’ circumstances outside the program.
      🔗
    • Make efforts to strengthen collaborative relationships and validations between students.
      🔗
    • Use positive affirmations and express confidence in students’ abilities to reach learning goals.
      🔗
    • Model respectful disagreement and show how to build on each other’s ideas.
      🔗
    • Break long silences that may cause uncertainty, down time, or loss of confidence.
      🔗
    • Give constructive, specific feedback; avoid general praise.
      🔗
    • Celebrate small successes to reinforce effort and progress.
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Students’ Perspectives.

    • “My mentor tells me that I can do it, and that helps a lot.”
      🔗
    • “I feel proud when my mentor notices what I figured out.”
      🔗
    • “It’s okay to make mistakes here; my mentor notices them and corrects me.”
      🔗
    • “I trust my mentor because they take time to help me.”
      🔗
    • “I feel like I can share my ideas without anyone judging me.”
      🔗
    • “I feel supported and have people to go to when I have problems.”
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Component 4: Be a role model

    Show students how to approach challenges, think critically, and behave in a responsible way.
    🔗

    Mentors’ Actions.

    • Model expected behavior and show appropriate ways to respond to challenges.
      🔗
    • Be active and visible — interrupt when needed, complement and correct when necessary, and show how to contribute meaningfully.
      🔗
    • Make connections between your life story and the students’ life stories.
      🔗
    • Think out loud frequently to demonstrate reasoning and problem-solving habits.
      🔗
    • Share your academic, professional, and life experiences with students to inspire them.
      🔗
    • Show humility and vulnerability by admitting mistakes and explaining what you learned from them.
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Students’ Perspectives.

    • “I like when my mentor shows how to think through problems out loud.”
      🔗
    • “I feel inspired when my mentor talks about their own school or work.”
      🔗
    • “My mentor shows us how to be respectful and handle challenges.”
      🔗
    • “I notice how my mentor keeps trying even when it’s hard.”
      🔗
    • “I want to be like my mentor because they stay positive and help everyone.”
      🔗

    Subsection Warm & Balanced Talk: 10 Things To Try!

    Avoid... Try Instead
    Mostly say “I will...” and “You will...”, thinking that students need to be taught without their input. Often say “We can...” — think of mentors and students as a team with “horizontal” interactions.
    Not saying mentors’ or students’ names. Always use names during conversation. (Tip: don’t be afraid of asking for names again!)
    Having long periods (10+ seconds) of awkward silence where people are confused. Keep it dynamic: everyone talks! Ask questions, rephrase. Use silence with a purpose (like think time).
    Having a weak or no reaction to a student’s idea. React strongly to ideas (show excitement, confusion, curiosity, build on it, etc.). (Tip: don’t always have the same reaction.)
    Acknowledging a student’s idea without saying their name. Acknowledge both the idea and the student who shared it.
    Talking for a long time without checking if others are engaged. Invite peers to “interrupt” or add to what you’re saying. Ask mentors directly to join in.
    Always asking questions only to “the students” as a group. Keep it fresh: ask all teammates, all mentors, a pair of students, or specific people.
    Never interrupt or add to what other mentors say. Respectfully interrupt teammates, adding energy and showing excitement.
    Letting only one or two voices dominate. Actively invite quieter students to share (doesn’t have to be orally), and create space for everyone’s input.
    Using only serious or academic talk. Mix humor, encouragement, and small personal shares to build connections.
    🔗

    Subsection Activity: Happy Triplets

    Look at the “10 Things to Try!” table. Carefully choose any two or three actions to try from the column on the right that look related in the sense that doing one can lead to doing the other two, or, how not doing one can lead to not doing the rest. Explain your thinking, describing all the relations that you foresee, when teaching, between the chosen actions. To close, write a general teaching principle that’s behind the actions that you selected.
    🔗
    The goal of this activity is to understand the general principles behind the actions, which can guide you when teaching why these actions matter.
