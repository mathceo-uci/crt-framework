    Section The Three Contexts: Structure of the Program, Pedagogical Practices, and Curriculum

    Subsection Structure of the Program

    This is the most fundamental context, as it sets the tone for everything else in terms of expectations. It encompasses the basics of how the program is run, the logistical elements, and the pedagogical goals. It also helps that good practices are sustained, and don’t depend necessarily on particular individuals.
    🔗
    The structure of the program includes elements like where the program is held, how often it runs, what kinds of people work with the program, which mathematical resources (such as manipulatives) can be provided, how are students organized (for example, do they work in small groups?), which nature of mentor-students relations will be pursued, which cognitive demand in the activities is expected, and what are the explicit and implicit expectations for mentor practices. The program structure is often designed without noticing how it might impact the student experience. Changing aspects of how the program is run can positively impact other activities and outcomes.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Pedagogical Practices

    While the curriculum lays the foundation for culturally responsive practice in a program, the pedagogical practices used when teaching the activities are what make those practices visible to the students. We can embed these practices in the activities, but unless they are activated when working with students, they cannot be executed at full capacity and to the fullest intended quality. So, mentors are responsible for making CRT alive and thus have the largest impact on the students.
    🔗
    Of particular importance is that often there are different groups of people designing activities, and implementing them. It is key that there is communication on what the goals of the activity are and what is important to do with the students. It is also key that mentors have the time to anticipate obstacles that could happen during the learning session, and how to prepare for them: the curriculum will provide some answers, but not all, and mentors will need to rely on their peers and a supporting program structure to succeed. (For example, useful assessments to gain conceptual knowledge, opportunities to learn about multiple representations, time to practice the session and anticipate misconceptions, time to think about their particular students and how they will react to the context, etc.)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Curriculum / Activity Design

    Core to any program is the design of the activities it runs. This is an essential place to encode culturally responsive practices because it sets the tone for everything that happens afterward. However, it is important to remember that the design of the curriculum is not everything — just because an activity includes certain practices it doesn’t mean they will be done in practice. In other words, a powerful curriculum far from guarantees that it will translate to excellence implementing it. An intentional effort must be made for this, which includes constant preparation. At that point it comes down to the program structure and pedagogical practices.
    🔗
    Conversely, starting with a curriculum that includes few opportunities for culturally relevant practices will most likely set low bar expectations for the mentors, and it will require a big effort for mentors to adjust and incorporate CRT. For this, we believe that it’s critical for the curriculum to include and show all the CRT goals that are expected, rather than expecting mentors to “fix” it on the fly. If the curriculum shows limitations, then it should be modified to address them, for next time it’s implemented.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Aligning the Three Contexts

    A culturally responsive after-school program will apply all six dimensions within each of the three contexts — each dimension must be realized to carry a practice all the way to student growth. For example, we could design an activity to foster interpersonal relationships between students and mentors, but if mentors are not trained to properly run the activity it may not perform as expected. Therefore, not only do we need to apply each dimension at each level, but also make sure that the efforts at each level are in concert. A key good practice in this regard is to document the goals and expectations at each context, see how they interact, and assess if they are realistic, or which changes need to be made. Reflection sessions should be held on a regular basis, as a way to effectively change practices.
    🔗
    One frequent error is making changes at each context but in different, disconnected or incompatible ways (sometimes even contradictory). You may still see some benefit in this case, but those changes are likely not as impactful as they could be, and may not be long term ones.
