Introduction

In this document, we present a framework that can help any afterschool program in developing culturally responsive teaching (CRT) in a sustained and coherent way, and that can keep track of progress made in the different components that comprise CRT, at different levels. This is far from a straightforward task, as it involves multiple efforts in different directions, constant reflection, and aligning all efforts. We hope that this document serves as a useful and practical guide to start this exciting process.

Our program began thinking about these dimensions of culturally responsive teaching to help understand how to categorize the ways we were adapting the program to culturally responsive practices. We wanted to develop a better knowledge of why certain practices are helpful and others are less so, and ways to explain the key important ideas that can be followed at different levels (leadership teams, curriculum development, training and coaching, mentoring observation, and assessment). Over time, we listed practices that we found beneficial, began to foster them, and saw which practices had similar goals or outcomes. This resulted in constructing the 6 Dimensions, which gives a general, coherent structure for the practices which we found were most impactful for students, and needed to be sustained at different levels.

The context of our program

We developed these dimensions within the context of UCI Math CEO, an after-school math program focused on LatinX youth in middle school, run on a university campus, and with undergraduate students serving as mentors who run activities with the students. Students usually work in small groups of 6-8 students and 2 mentors. The program leadership developed the content of each activity and trained mentors beforehand, in person, on how to run the activities, the goal of each activity, and general teaching practices. The training includes virtual homeworks, as well as weekly reflection about the pedagogical practices.

1. Students at the center

This dimension is mentoring focused on students as individuals having a distinct culture and identity, and developing the activities, the teaching methods, the pace, and everything else based on how students are responding. Students, individually, drive everything else! To activate this, it is essential to value active participation and give voice to students, using a learn by doing approach to continuously improve our understanding of how students learn best, their strengths, and their interests.

One of our programs’ goals is allow students to direct their own learning according to their skills and interests. Rather than having a set list of very specific objectives for the students to learn, the program activities are designed to have general objectives that can be satisfied in multiple ways. Students do not need to complete every single task, but rather the tasks are guidelines for how to scaffold the understanding −students are free to explore the activities and end up doing one that is sufficiently challenging for them.

At the same time, we do not want students to work totally alone −we want students to balance working out their own answers with learning with other students. Not only does this reinforce teamwork and social skills, but this also has students sharing knowledge with each other. This has two benefits, (1) that students are encouraged to practice sharing and explaining their knowledge in a way that makes sense to other students, and (2) that students can learn from each other. This second one is important since mentors understand activities differently than the students, so the explanation that makes the most sense to a student may naturally come from another student. In this way we can maintain the focus on students by having them help each other rather than immediately having the mentor intervene.

2. Interpersonal relationships and well-being

This dimension entails promoting positive relations between mentors and students, and among students. The process starts with mentors’ actions and dispositions, which lead to a safe environment that allows for relationships to grow. Mentors have a “beyond teacher” mindset, and actively look for opportunities to connect with students at a deeper level in a two-way relationship.

One important difference between the after-school and in-school settings is that in an after-school program there is potentially more room to recognize the individuality of the student and support them holistically, in many different dimensions. One way this manifests is learning about the student −the idea is that we learn about the student at a deeper level than what they do mathematically or in school.

We have found that students create deep bonds with the mentors they work with (which takes some time), and see them as a source of external support. In this way, mentors teach, by modeling, socio-emotional skills to students in addition to mathematical skills. In fact, understanding the student at this deeper level makes students more comfortable challenging themselves and lets mentors draw out more mathematical skill from students. Students are also more inclined to share personal challenging or traumatic events with mentors, who can then relay critical information to program leaders and teachers to make sure students get the support they need.

Mentors’ beliefs and attitudes

  • I believe that every student has unique strengths that deserve celebration.
  • I believe that building trust and care is as important as teaching content.
  • I believe that mistakes can turn into opportunities for growth, not signs of failure.
  • I believe that listening carefully to students helps me understand their perspectives.
  • I believe that learning is more meaningful when students feel supported.
  • I believe that when I model skills, it inspires students to think deeply about themselves.
  • I believe that all students can achieve learning goals.
  • I believe that being approachable makes students feel safe to take risks.
  • I believe that reflecting on experiences fosters personal growth.
  • I believe that celebrating small successes motivates students to challenge themselves.

Mentors’ actions

  • Welcome Students
    Use your words and actions to create a positive, welcoming space where meaningful learning can happen.

  1. Greet students individually when they arrive to make them feel seen.
  2. Smile, laugh, and make eye contact throughout the session.
  3. Use positive gestures that are culturally appropriate, relevant, and respectful to show care and excitement (examples: pat on the back, high five, fist pumps—always ask first if unsure).
  4. Listen carefully to students’ ideas, respond with enthusiasm, and celebrate and acknowledge their contributions.
  5. Learn and consistently use students’ names the way they want to be called.
  6. Interact with care, respect, and compassion, and react to disrespectful behavior.

Students’ perspectives

  • I like coming here because my mentor always makes me feel welcome.
  • I feel happy when we start and everyone says hi.
  • My mentor remembers my name, and that makes me feel special.
  • I feel safe because the mentor is nice and positive.
  • It’s fun when the mentor jokes around and smiles with us.
  • I can share about my own interests and experiences
  • I feel safe and respected during the program
  • I feel that my mentors care about me
  • I feel that my ideas and suggestions are valued by everyone
  • Find Ways to Connect
    Actions that encourage meaningful, two-way exchanges with students.
  1. Show through words and body language that you are approachable, responsive, and care about each student.
  2. Share small, relatable stories or experiences to connect with students.
  3. Remember and refer to students’ personal stories, interests, and feelings.
  4. Check in regularly on students’ progress and struggles, offering emotional support.
  • My mentor actually listens when I talk and remembers stuff I said before.
  • I feel like they really care about what’s going on in my life.
  • I can tell my mentor about things I like and they get it.
  • I like it when we talk about stuff beyond the content of the meeting.
  • I feel comfortable asking questions because my mentor is friendly.
  • My mentor shares interesting stories with us!
  • Build confidence and trust
    Help students trust themselves, you, and the learning process.
  1. Be mindful of students’ circumstances outside the program.
  2. Make efforts to strengthen collaborative relationships and validations between students.
  3. Use positive affirmations and express confidence in students’ abilities to reach learning goals.
  4. Model respectful disagreement and show how to build on each other’s ideas.
  5. Break long silences that may cause uncertainty, down time, or loss of confidence.
  6. Give constructive, specific feedback; avoid general praise.
  7. Celebrate small successes to reinforce effort and progress.
  • My mentor tells me that I can do it, and that helps a lot.
  • I feel proud when my mentor notices what I figured out.
  • It’s okay to make mistakes here; my mentor notices them and corrects me.
  • I trust my mentor because they take time to help me.
  • I feel like I can share my ideas without anyone judging me.
  • I feel supported and have people to go to when I have problems
  • Be a Role Model
    Show students how to approach challenges, think critically, and behave in a responsible way.
  1. Model expected behavior and show appropriate ways to respond to challenges.
  2. Be active and visible—interrupt when needed, complement and correct when necessary, and show how to contribute meaningfully.
  3. Make connections between your life story and the students’ life stories.
  4. Think out loud frequently to demonstrate reasoning and problem-solving habits.
  5. Share your academic, professional, and life experiences with students to inspire them.
  6. Show humility and vulnerability by admitting mistakes and explaining what you learned from them.
  • I like when my mentor shows how to think through problems out loud.
  • I feel inspired when my mentor talks about their own school or work.
  • My mentor shows us how to be respectful and handle challenges.
  • I notice how my mentor keeps trying even when it’s hard.
  • I want to be like my mentor because they stay positive and help everyone.

3. Deep thinking

This dimension drives the design of disciplinary and enrichment activities, and sets the expectation that both the curriculum and the mentors should demand high level thinking from students, so that they are reasonably challenged, learn new concepts, go beyond procedures, and use holistic thinking, logical reasoning and critical thinking, including modeling. Thinking questions are used for advancing understanding.

Many students begin the program with a negative attitude about their mathematical skill and math overall. It is common for students to feel so poorly about math that their perception of themselves prevents them from developing their mathematical and logical thinking. One of the first things that a mentor needs to do when working with students is to establish a genuine belief in their mathematical abilities. This also links with the mentor developing a relationship with the student, this relationship makes the student believe in their mentor’s opinions and therefore establishes a belief in themself.

In line with actively believing in the student’s ability and knowledge, we then have high expectations for students both inside and outside the program. This does not mean that we expect students to fully understand and master every activity, rather that students engage with every activity thinking that they are capable of understanding and mastering the activity, and they make the most out of it. In that setting we can challenge students beyond their normal math education, and oftentimes our curriculum brings new mathematical ideas that can bring an extra challenge and amplify student’s perceptions about the scope of mathematics.

Mentors’ beliefs and attitudes

  • I believe that every student can reason deeply when given careful guidance.
  • I believe that understanding comes from connecting ideas, patterns, and examples.
  • I believe that students’ thinking should be visible, celebrated, and discussed.
  • I believe that asking thoughtful questions helps students discover connections.
  • I believe that multiple approaches to a topic strengthen reasoning and help build flexible thinking.
  • I believe that persistence through challenges develops confidence and deeper understanding.
  • I believe that scaffolding and modeling help students reason more clearly.
  • I believe that encouraging students to justify their answers fosters responsibility and careful thinking.
  • I believe that connecting learning to students’ experiences makes abstract ideas more meaningful.
  • I believe that creating a supportive reasoning community motivates students to share and learn from peers.

Students’ perspectives

  • I like that the tasks are hard but not impossible.
  • I know what we’re trying to learn, and it makes sense.
  • I feel ready because the questions help me think step by step.
  • I can start problems without getting totally confused, thanks to the way they explain.
  • I feel like I actually understand the topic, not just memorize stuff.
  • Pave the way for deep thinking
    Create a learning environment to encourage students to reason, notice patterns, and connect ideas.
  1. Use multiple examples and contrasting cases and situations (with variability) to build intuition, reveal patterns, and uncover misconceptions.
  2. Ask students to compare and contrast different contexts or real-world situations to deepen understanding.
  3. Encourage students to justify their answers and help them explain or uncover their assumptions.
  4. Ask questions that gently, but firmly, challenge students’ current understanding.
  5. Promote, mostly, elaborated answers instead of brief or yes/no or closed responses.
  6. Encourage multiple reasoning paths to justify claims, positions, or answers.
  7. Illustrate intentional mistakes or narrow perspectives that highlight gaps in reasoning, and discuss them.
  8. Foster peer discussions where students analyze and critique each other’s reasoning.

  • I feel good when I can see more than one way to solve it.

  • I like comparing my solution with friends and seeing different ideas.
  • Support students’ attempts at reasoning

  1. Give students chances to make their thinking visible (not just the answer) through speaking, writing, charts, diagrams, or other representations.
  2. Help students abstract core ideas and concepts in different levels of complexity.
  3. Offer explicit reasoning support, including sentence frames, modeling, and worked examples that include explicit reasoning.
  4. Address misconceptions through prompts, questions, and guided exploration.
  5. Celebrate partial reasoning or creative approaches to encourage risk-taking.
  6. Encourage students to explain their reasoning to peers, fostering accountability and clarity.
  7. Give feedback that focuses on the thinking process rather than only the final answer.

  • I feel supported when they show me a way to explain my reasoning.

  2. Offer strategic hints or new questions, rather than giving answers outright.

  4. Adjust challenges to ensure productive struggle, not absolute frustration.
  5. Support students in setting personal goals to push their thinking further.

  • It feels good to finally solve a tricky problem by myself.

A Case Study: Multiplications in Egypt (powers of 2)

In this activity, students make sense of a particular method for multiplication from ancient Egypt (“duplication”).

In this method, we double one of the multiplicands repeatedly, record the results on a table, and then add some of them to find the answer. To see which of them, students think about powers of 2 (1, 2, 4, 8, …) and binary representations, which are the main concepts of the activity.

Example: 19 is 1 + 2 + 16, and so 19 x 213 is found adding the values in rows #1, #2, and #5.

Students will investigate if this method just works sometimes, if we are “lucky”, or if it always works. This naturally leads them to investigate whether every positive integer is equal to a sum of powers of 2.

This activity is accessible for many students from the start, but allows for deep thinking and logical reasoning, and generalizations or extensions. Let’s go through some to see how a mentor can excel in teaching it!

Actions

Prepare for deep content and relational structures

Get ready so that activities are geared toward deep reasoning.

This activity is accessible, as all students can engage with the computations, and use the table to explain their thinking. As the activity progresses, more reasoning opportunities open up.

Mentors can plan this activity keeping the following points in mind:

  • The first tasks relate to the distributive property, “a(b+c) = ab + ac”, and should use it to justify why the Egyptian method works.
  • Although not all values of the table will be used in the final sum, it’s important not to skip any because to find each new value, you need to know the previous one.
  • When finding a times b, you can choose any of the two numbers to double, but it’s probably best to pick the largest one. This is because the other number is the one whose binary decomposition we need to find. Students may be confused about this.
  • Students should realize that the method is particularly fast if one of the multiplicands is a power of 2.
  • Students should connect the question “does this method always work?” with “is every positive integer a sum of powers of 2?” The answer is: yes. The heart of the activity will be convincing ourselves that this is the case. Students may think that sometimes it’s not possible.
  • A key insight to see the main result has to do with thinking inductively. No need to formally introduce mathematical induction, but rather to explain it informally and use it in particular examples. (E.g: Suppose that we check for numbers up to 99. To check for 100: find the largest power of 2 below, 64, and consider 100 − 64 = X. So 100 = X + 64, and thus it’s a sum of powers of 2 as well.) Another way is to show binary notation and how adding a unit each time is similar to decimal notation (ex: 999+1 = 1000, a reset. Similarly in binary 1111 + 1 = 1
  • Students may confuse powers of 2 with multiples of 2. Pay attention.
  • Students may think that 1 is not a power of 2, but it’s the “first” one, and it’s needed always for the odd numbers (you can reason why).

Mentors can keep these high-level learning goals (and communicate them through the session in different ways):

  • Students should describe the Egyptian method in their own words.
  • Students should practice mental math when doubling values, and number sense when finding the powers of two that add to a number. For example, strategically start with the highest possible power of two that does not go past the number.
  • Students should convince themselves that every positive integer is a sum of powers of two. While a formal proof is not needed, a good intuition and explanation should happen during the session. Binary representations will be helpful. Advanced students can also think about the uniqueness (the sum is unique).

Mentors can plan thinking questions ahead. Here are examples:

  • “Can we choose any of the numbers to be the initial value in the table? If so, which is more convenient? Give me examples.”
  • “Why does the method work? Can you write an equation?”
  • “What would we need for the method to always work?”
  • “When trying to express this number as a sum of powers of two, which strategy did you use?”
  • “Do we need, sometimes, more than one instance of a particular power of two? Why?”
  • “39 is 32 + 4 + 2 + 1. Can you use this to find 40 as a sum of powers of 2? How? How about from an even number Y to Y+1?”
  • Suppose that we know that X is a sum of powers of 2, so it has a “code” of 0’s and 1’s. Can you use this to see if X+1 is also a sum of powers of 2? What can the new code look like?

Mentors can prepare supports to help illustrate logical connections, such as:

  • Analogies between the decimal system and the binary system.
  • Use of fingers to express binary numbers in a fast and playful way, by matching fingers to powers of 2 in a specific order.
  • A “binary mat” that helps represent numbers as sums of powers of two, where students can place tokens in “active” powers of 2 that are used in the sum. This manipulative allows us to reason inductively about sums of powers of 2, and to make sense of the more abstract binary representation.


  • An analogy to money: we have weird bills, one of each: $1, $2, $4, $8, $16, …, $128. Then we can buy anything costing an integer value and we don’t need change! (Ask to check that students see that we can buy things up to $255.)

Pave the way for deep thinking

Create a learning environment to encourage students to reason, notice patterns, and connect ideas.

Here are cases and examples that mentors can use to build intuition, reveal patterns, and uncover misconceptions:

  • For a given product XY, use the table in two ways: first, starting with X, and then starting with Y. Discuss which was more convenient.
  • Have students see the relation between the binary sums for two consecutive numbers (X, X+1)
  • Explore what happens to a binary sum when we double a number (it just “shifts”) (X, 2X)

Mentors can ask students to compare and contrast different contexts or real-world situations to deepen understanding.

  • Discuss the trick for multiplying by 4 (doubling twice), or multiplying by 8 (doubling 3 times), and relate this to the Egyptian table.
  • Discuss about bits and bytes, and connect to the context of computers and represent information using binary codes.
  • Use a “decimal” table (similar to the usual base 10 algorithm) to find a product, to help understand the Egyptian method. (Example: 342 x 7)

Mentors can encourage students to justify their answers and help them explain or uncover their assumptions. In particular:

  • Have students justify why we should not add all the rows in the second column.
  • If students answer that the method always works, have students realize that they are assuming that we can always express numbers as sums of powers of two, and that we should try to convince ourselves that this is always possible.

Mentors should ask questions to challenge students’ understanding. For example:

  • If students seem to learn the method, but not why it works, ask them how they know that the answer is, indeed, the value of the product.
  • If students are unsure about which first steps could be used to express a number as a sum of powers of two, guide them to start with a number (say, 50), look for the largest power of 2 below if (32), and write 50 as 32 plus something (32 + 18), and then repeat the steps for 18, to realize a way.

After yes/no or closed responses, mentors should follow up with questions or prompts. For example:

  • If students say that it’s obvious that the method works, ask them to write equations to convince you (they should use the distributive property).
  • If students say that we can choose any number as the initial value for the table, ask them why, and if there’s an advantage for picking one number instead of the other one.
  • If students say that every positive integer is a sum of powers of 2, ask them to convince you of this in general. Help them reason with a general case, for example going from X to X+1 (using binary notation is useful).

Mentors can encourage various reasoning paths to justify their conclusions. This is particularly helpful to see why every positive integer is a sum of powers of 2. Include these paths (some have more generality, but the others are still useful to build intuition and relational knowledge):

  • Do lots of examples and see how one always succeeds. (As you do this, check for patterns.)
  • Informal induction: See how from the binary representation of X, one can produce the one for X+1 (look for the first “0”: it will turn into a 1, and the “1”’s before will reset to 0. This involves lots of discussions.
  • Informal induction: Do two cases: if X is odd, then you just add a 1 to the 2^0 position for X−1. If X is even, then look at Y = X/2, and see how you can produce the new binary sequence from there.
  • “Removing” the largest power of 2: Start with X, and look for the largest power of 2 that is less or equal to X. Subtract it from X to get Y. If Y=0 or you can easily build the sum for Y, then the sum for X is easy. Otherwise, repeat the action: look for the largest power of 2 that is less or equal to Y, and subtract it.

Illustrate intentional mistakes or narrow perspectives that highlight gaps in reasoning, and discuss them.

Foster peer discussions where students analyze and critique each other’s reasoning.

4. Access and Relevance

This dimension entails setting learning activities in contexts which are relevant and meaningful to students, using (formal and informal) language familiar with students, and adopting multiple modalities (manipulatives, diagrams, tables, pictures, and more abstract representations) to help students advance their understanding. We aim for students to be actively engaged using different modalities and gain ownership of them.

A key part of making relevant and engaging activities is to make the content relatable to the students. One aspect of this is applicable to mathematics as a whole, that there needs to be a visible connection between the abstract math concepts and the everyday reality of students. This is important for students to be able to build upon their own formal and informal learned experiences (funds of knowledge).

An important and critical aspect is making sure that the math situations make sense to each  individual student. This means that mathematical interactions should be tailored to the knowledge and interests of the student. What makes sense to one student may not be useful to another student so mentors must be prepared with multiple ways to introduce a problem, as well as multiple tools that can lead them to understand it and, ultimately, solve it. This includes using a large range of concepts, representations, and interactive components, and knowing how to move between them, to make visible connections (for example, moving from diagram to table, or from certain spatial manipulative to writing numerical expressions).

In many activities one objective is to introduce students to certain mathematical terms, and teach them to reason based on those terms. The natural inclination is then to push students to use this terminology when thinking about a problem and to be precise in their work, but this can often be counterproductive. Just as we need to connect concepts to real world situations for them to be relevant to students, using common language the students are comfortable with is important to their understanding. After establishing their understanding of the concepts they can translate to more formal language, gradually. Language can also be an integral part of cultural identity, so allowing students to speak other languages or use slang makes the content more relatable and shows that they are a valued part of the program in whatever way they choose to participate. “Slang is not an obstacle for understanding, but rather an advantage!”

5. Equity through embracing cultural diversity

This dimension focuses on how to honor culture (starting with students’ and mentors’ cultural funds of knowledge) and use it to build an equitable community of mentors and mentees. Curriculum and mentors’ practices are designed to facilitate cultural exchanges and celebrate diversity. Implicit biases and manifestations of inequities, marginalization, (lack of) privilege or representation, and cultural appropriation are exposed and openly discussed.

There are two components to exposing students to cultural diversity, one being affirming a student’s cultural identity and the other being showing them a broader range of cultures. The first point is especially important when working with under-represented populations. Often, activities are implicitly presented with examples from the dominant culture which can make them less useful to these students and can make them feel isolated. The answer is to intentionally craft activities that allow students to feel validated in their cultural identity and allows them to share their culture with others.

Similarly, we want to expose students to other cultures so they can in turn appreciate other cultures, while reaffirming their identities, and finding common grounds and connections. This includes mathematical practices that give a different approach to doing math. However, you do need to take care to learn enough about the cultures and at a deep enough level to avoid cultural appropriation, or falling in stereotypes. Even details such as the pronunciation of different words is important, as well as presenting original sources of knowledge.

In terms of mathematics teaching and learning, culture and decolonization also plays a role: it means not seeing the student as someone with deficits that needs to be tutored to fix a gap, but rather as a complex human being with lots of mathematical potential to be realized, through high expectations. It also means that mentors are also learning from the students.

  • I believe students deserve role models who reflect their identities and inspire them.
  • I believe learning is deeper when it connects to music, stories, traditions, and knowledge from many cultures.

  • I believe respectful conversations about differences can help students grow as thoughtful and caring citizens.

6. Promoting social justice

This dimension aims to increase awareness about social justice issues through mathematical activities, analyzing the causes of the problems and brainstorming ideas to make progress on solving them, starting with personal stories from students, and moving to larger domains such as families, communities, the country, and the world.

In our program, some of the mathematical activities include opportunities to raise awareness about different social justice issues that can relate at different levels to student’s personal situations. These can be presented as games or simulations in which systemic injustice can emerge, showing real data that speaks about inequity, or through fictional situations that can later connect to the real world. We use scaffolded questions (sequences of 2-3 carefully crafted questions) that gradually increase the depth of discussions, first involving student’s experiences, then trying to capture the social issue more generally, and including key concepts and terms, and then giving space to reflections and connection to the mathematics learned.

The peak of any activity is to connect to equity and social justice in deep ways such that the mathematics explored plays a key role in the discussion. This is quite challenging to do and likely won’t happen in every activity, but this is the ultimate way for students to better understand the world around them and the ways that math can address real world social problems. Part of this is similar to holding students to high expectations where we want to empower students to believe that they are capable of making a difference, even if that happens in the future, or happens gradually. We are building awareness, at the very least.

The fundamental goal:
Personal Development & Growth

The center of the diagram represents the personal growth of the students, and is centrally located as that is the fundamental, or ultimate, goal of the program. Accordingly, the six dimensions all flow to the middle as these dimensions act as guides for what the program needs to do to facilitate student growth. Around personal development are three rings: program structure, pedagogical practices, and curriculum. These represent the three contexts in which we implement the six dimensions when building a culturally responsive program. An effective program that supports student growth is one that embraces all six dimensions and carefully implements them in each of the three contexts.

In this program, a particular way to promote personal development is by connecting with the goal of going to college. Since the program runs on a university campus, students see what a university looks like and how it runs. Mentors are undergraduate students who can share information about their classes and other aspects of being a university student. The goal is not to push students to choose a college-bound path, but instead encourage them that they are capable of choosing that path if they want, and help start developing it.

The three contexts:
Structure of the program, pedagogical practices, and curriculum

Structure of the program

This is the most fundamental context, as it sets the tone for everything else in terms of expectations. It encompasses the basics of how the program is run, the logistical elements, and the pedagogical goals. It also helps that good practices are sustained, and don’t depend necessarily on particular individuals.

The structure of the program includes elements like where the program is held, how often it runs, what kinds of people work with the program, which mathematical resources (such as manipulatives) can be provided, how are students organized (for example, do they work in small groups?), which nature of mentor-students relations will be pursued, which cognitive demand in the activities is expected, and what are the explicit and implicit expectations for mentor practices. The program structure is often designed without noticing how it might impact the student experience. Changing aspects of how the program is run can positively impact other activities and outcomes.

Pedagogical Practices

While the curriculum lays the foundation for culturally responsive practice in a program, the pedagogical practices used when teaching the activities are what make those practices visible to the students. We can embed these practices in the activities, but unless they are activated when working with students, they cannot be executed at full capacity and to the fullest intended quality. So, mentors are responsible for making CRT alive and thus have the largest impact on the students.

Of particular importance is that often there are different groups of people designing activities, and implementing them. It is key that there is communication on what the goals of the activity are and what is important to do with the students. It is also key that mentors have the time to anticipate obstacles that could happen during the learning session, and how to prepare for them: the curriculum will provide some answers, but not all, and mentors will need to rely on their peers and a supporting program structure to succeed. (For example, useful assessments to gain conceptual knowledge, opportunities to learn about multiple representations, time to practice the session and anticipate misconceptions, time to think about their particular students and how they will react to the context, etc.)

Curriculum / Activity Design

Core to any program is the design of the activities it runs. This is an essential place to encode culturally responsive practices because it sets the tone for everything that happens afterward. However, it is important to remember that the design of the curriculum is not everything −just because an activity includes certain practices it doesn’t mean they will be done in practice. In other works, a powerful curriculum far from guarantees that it will translate to excellence implementing it. An intentional effort must be made for this, which includes constant preparation. At that point it comes down to the program structure and pedagogical practices.

Conversely, starting with a curriculum that includes few opportunities for culturally relevant practices will most likely set low bar expectations for the mentors, and it will require a big effort for mentors to adjust and incorporate CRT. For this, we believe that it’s critical for the curriculum to include and show all the CRT goals that are expected, rather than expecting mentors to “fix” it on the fly. If the curriculum shows limitations, then it should be modified to address them, for next time it’s implemented.

Aligning the three contexts

A culturally responsive after-school program will apply all six dimensions within each of the three contexts - each dimension must be realized to carry a practice all the way to student growth. For example, we could design an activity to foster interpersonal relationships between students and mentors, but if mentors are not trained to properly run the activity it may not perform as expected. Therefore, not only do we need to apply each dimension at each level, but also make sure that the efforts at each level are in concert. A key good practice in this regard is to document the goals and expectations at each context, see how they interact, and assess if they are realistic, or which changes need to be made. Reflection sessions should be held on a regular basis, as a way to effectively change practices.

One frequent error is making changes at each context but in different, disconnected or incompatible ways (sometimes even contradictory). You may still see some benefit in this case, but those changes are likely not as impactful as they could be, and may not be long term ones.

 

Applying the Dimensions

When beginning to create a culturally responsive program, the key place to start is with establishing interpersonal relationships between students and mentors. This is important to start as early as possible because it sets the foundation for the rest of the dimensions. After that, the next three dimensions are the focus since adding equity and social justice content can be quite challenging depending on the subject area. One final recommendation is to plan out how these dimensions can be upheld in different parts of the program, such as designing curriculum, training mentors/volunteers, and when the activities are implemented. There are lots of ways to implement the dimensions so it is quite important to make sure that goals and priorities are in line at each step of the process.

Possible Modifications to the Six Dimensions

We recognize that each after school program has a very different nature and different ways to achieve similar goals. Therefore, you may find that certain elements of the six dimensions don’t apply 100% to your setting. However, we believe that most concepts are sufficiently general and can be applied to a broader range of programs with minimal modifications. It is also possible that you want one of the dimensions to have more weight than others, and make efforts to develop them more.

The six dimensions were developed with a mathematics program in mind, and the context of a math program is prevalent in dimensions 3, Deep Critical and Logical Thinking, 4, Relevance, Friendly Language and Representations, and 6, Promoting social justice. The first contains much of the goals of the program, thinking of math skills as a kind of critical thinking skill. This can be replaced by a dimension based on the goals of a specific program, with consideration for what makes that subject cognitively challenging for students and which core thinking skills need to be developed. Students’ attitudes and perceptions of the subject can also be a part of this dimension.

Dimension 3 covers the ways we show, communicate and discuss math with students. Relevance means tying the activity to something the students experience in their everyday life, and friendly language and representations means we should be accommodating with students using other languages, slang, or other informal language to answer questions and we provide multiple ways (modalities) to illustrate mathematical concepts. In other subjects or fields, this can be replaced with whatever makes communicating the subject concepts challenging, and using content pedagogical knowledge to specify which access points are useful to include all students into making sense of the learning situations. This may involve stories, specific manipulatives, simulations, the use of certain diagrams, and so on.

Dimension 6 includes discussing how the mathematics learned can connect to social justice issues. This can be done in many specific ways such as:

  • exploring fairness and equity using fractions and proportions (to make sense of unfairness in numerical ways),
  • developing projects that involve numerical operations, geometry, graph theory, or other math to make social justice visible in concrete cases,
  • modeling systems with mathematics that involve injustice depending on the parameters,
  • playing and analyzing logical games that explore fairness and unfairness,
  • using probability to explore social outcomes that are associated with privilege, and exploring data to identify lack of diversity and representation.

In programs focused on a different subject (such as history, arts, biology, chemistry, etc.), much of the same ideas still apply, but, naturally, the particular ways in which the content subject connects and promotes social justice may change. We suggest the content experts find and keep track of those strong aspects that can naturally lead to topics related to social justice.
